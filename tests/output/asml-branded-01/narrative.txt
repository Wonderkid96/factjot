1. Clean water from a faucet seems effortless today, yet controlling water has defined human civilization for millennia. Societies that mastered water thrived while those that failed collapsed. Currently, seven in ten people have access to running water, though this security masks deeper vulnerabilities.

2. Cape Town faces an unprecedented crisis as Day Zero approaches—the date when the city must shut off water to four million residents. They will receive rationed water and queue at distribution stations instead. This catastrophe reveals that modern infrastructure cannot guarantee water security.

3. Cape Town is not alone in this danger. São Paulo, Melbourne, Jakarta, London, Beijing, Istanbul, Tokyo, Bangalore, Barcelona, and Mexico City will all face similar crises within decades unless water consumption changes dramatically. By 2040, most of the world will lack sufficient water to meet yearly demand.

4. Earth contains 326 million trillion gallons of water, yet this abundance masks a critical scarcity. Ninety-seven percent is saltwater and two percent is frozen at the poles, leaving only one percent for all human survival. Access to this one percent depends entirely on geography and wealth.

5. Mexico City exemplifies how poor water management creates crisis. The Spanish drained the Aztec lakes centuries ago, and the city now floods despite receiving more rain than London. The city must pipe water from distant regions or pump unsustainable amounts from underground aquifers.

6. Mexico City's groundwater extraction causes aquifers to deplete faster than they can refill over millennia. The city withdraws fifty percent of its water supply from local aquifers, risking half its supply within thirty to fifty years. This extraction also compresses soil, causing the city to sink nine inches annually in some areas.

7. Humans use vastly more water for agriculture and industry than for drinking and hygiene. A single hamburger requires 1,650 liters of water to produce, while a cotton shirt needs 2,500 liters. As global diets shift toward meat-heavy American patterns, water demand becomes impossible to sustain.

8. Water pricing remains artificially low because farmers pay almost nothing for it, hiding the true cost of production. Southern California grows water-intensive alfalfa in the desert using Colorado River water, paying less than delivery costs. Recognizing water's true value could redirect agriculture away from arid regions and toward sustainable practices.
